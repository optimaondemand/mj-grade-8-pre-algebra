📚 VOCABULARY

Congruence: Congruence means that two shapes are exactly the same in size and shape. This means you could move one shape over the other, and they would fit perfectly on top of each other without stretching or compressing.

Dilation: Dilation is a transformation that changes the size of a shape but keeps its proportions the same. It either makes the shape larger or smaller using a specific point called the center of dilation and a multiplier known as the scale factor.

Image: The image is the new shape that you get after you have transformed the original shape (called the preimage) by moving, resizing, or rotating it. Think of it as the result or outcome of the transformation.

Preimage: The preimage is the original shape before any transformations are applied to it. It's the starting point from which we create the image through transformations like translation, reflection, or dilation.

Reflection: A reflection is like placing a mirror along a line and flipping a shape over that line. The shape looks the same but is reversed, like seeing your mirror image.

Rigid Transformation: A rigid transformation changes the position of a shape without altering its size or shape. Types of rigid transformations include translations, rotations, and reflections. These transformations maintain the congruence between the preimage and the image.

Rotation: A rotation turns a shape around a fixed point, called the center of rotation, through a specific angle and direction (like clockwise or counterclockwise). The shape stays the same size and shape; it just spins around.

Scale Factor: The scale factor is a number that you multiply by the dimensions of the original shape (preimage) to get the dimensions of the new shape (image) during a dilation. A scale factor greater than 1 makes the shape larger, and a scale factor less than 1 makes it smaller.

Similarity: Two shapes are similar if they have the same shape but different sizes. This means they can be resized versions of each other, maintaining the proportions of their sides and angles. Similarity is achieved through transformations like dilation.

Translation: A translation moves every point of a shape the same distance in the same direction. The shape looks exactly the same, just in a different place. It's like sliding a shape across the floor or shifting it up, down, left, or right.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

In today's lesson, we will explore the concept of dilation, a transformation distinct from the translations, reflections, and rotations we have previously studied. Dilation involves resizing a figure on a flat plane, either making it larger or smaller without altering its shape. This transformation plays a crucial role in understanding proportions and scale in everyday contexts such as map reading, architecture, and art. We will learn how to determine if a dilation is greater than 1 or less than 1 and how to precisely calculate the scale factor between the preimage and the image using the lengths of corresponding sides.


When studying transformations in geometry, we often refer to the original figure as the "preimage" and the altered figure as the "image." This terminology helps us track how a figure changes through various transformations such as dilation, translation, reflection, or rotation. For clarity and precision in mathematical notation, when a point or figure is transformed, the new position is typically denoted with prime notation. For example, if a point on a preimage is labeled A, then after transformation, its corresponding point on the image is labeled A' (read as "A prime").


Understanding Dilations

Dilation is a transformation that changes the size of a figure but preserves its shape. The scale factor determines how much the figure is enlarged or reduced:

  • Dilation Greater than 1: When the scale factor is greater than 1, the image is larger than the preimage. This is called an enlargement. For example, if the scale factor is 2, every length in the preimage is doubled in the image.


Example 1

The preimage ABCD has been dilated to create the image A'B'C'D'.

In this example, the dilation is greater than 1 since the image is larger than the preimage (original).



  • Dilation Less than 1: When the scale factor is less than 1, the image is smaller than the preimage. This is known as a reduction. For example, a scale factor of 0.5 means every length in the preimage is halved in the image.


Example 2

The preimage FGHI was dilated to create the image F'G'H'I'.

In this example, the dilation is less than 1 since the image is smaller than the preimage (original).



Calculating the Scale Factor

To find the scale factor between a preimage and an image, you can use the lengths of corresponding sides:

  • Formula for Scale Factor: The scale factor k can be calculated by dividing the length of a side in the image by the corresponding side length in the preimage, expressed as:
    • \(\Large k=\frac{length\; of\; side\; in\; the\; image}{length\; of\; side\; in\; the\; preimage}\)


Example 3

The preimage KLM was dilated to create the image K'L'M'. Find the scale factor used to dilate the preimage.

  • To find the scale factor, we need to think about what the side length in the preimage was multiplied by to get the side length in the image. Since the image is bigger than the preimage, the scale factor will be greater than 1.
  • To calculate the scale factor we divide the length given in the image by the corresponding length in the preimage.
    • \(k=\frac{15}{6} \implies k=\frac{5}{2}\; or\; 2.5\)
  • We can express the scale factor as a fraction or as a decimal. Using the calculation, we find that the scale factor is \(\frac{5}{2}\) units or 2.5 units.


Example 4

The preimage KLM was dilated to create image K'L'M'. Find the scale factor used to create the image.

  • To find the scale factor, we need to think about what the side length in the preimage was multiplied by to get the side length in the image. Since the image is smaller than the preimage, the scale factor will be less than 1.
  • To calculate the scale factor we divide the length given in the image by the corresponding length in the preimage.
    • \(k=\frac{5.2}{8} \implies k=0.65\)
  • We can express the scale factor as a fraction or as a decimal. Since one of the side lengths was presented as a decimal, it is easier to calculate it as a decimal. Using the calculation, we find that the scale factor is 0.65 units.



Conclusion

Today, we've learned about dilations, a transformation that either enlarges or reduces a figure while maintaining its shape. Unlike translations, reflections, and rotations—which preserve both shape and size—dilations alter the size of the figure according to a specific scale factor. By understanding how to calculate this scale factor using the lengths of corresponding sides, you can apply this knowledge to real-world scenarios, enhancing your understanding of proportion and scale in a variety of fields. Practice these concepts by identifying transformations in objects around you and consider how scale impacts both function and design.
