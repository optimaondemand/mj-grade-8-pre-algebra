📚 VOCABULARY

Center of Rotation: The fixed point around which a figure turns during a rotation.

Coordinate Plane: A two-dimensional surface formed by the intersection of a horizontal number line (x-axis) and a vertical number line (y-axis).

Dilation: A transformation that changes the size of a figure but not its shape, either enlarging or reducing it based on a scale factor.

Image: The resulting figure after a transformation is applied to the original figure.

Origin: The point where the x-axis and y-axis intersect on the coordinate plane, located at (0,0).

Preimage: The original figure before any transformation has been applied.

Reflection: A transformation that "flips" a figure over a line, creating a mirror image.

Rigid Transformation: A transformation that changes a figure’s position but not its size or shape (translations, rotations, and reflections are rigid transformations).

Rotation: A transformation that turns a figure around a fixed point, called the center of rotation.

Scale Factor: A number that describes how much a figure is enlarged or reduced during a dilation.

Translation: A transformation that slides a figure from one location to another without turning or flipping it.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

In today’s lesson, we will explore how to identify and draw dilations on the coordinate plane. A dilation changes the size of a figure while keeping its shape. It can make a figure larger (an enlargement) or smaller (a reduction), and in 8th grade, these dilations will always happen from the origin (0, 0). You won’t need to use algebraic rules—instead, you’ll learn to recognize patterns in how the coordinates change and describe what’s happening geometrically and verbally. You’ll also get to practice drawing dilated figures using a scale factor, the number that tells you how much bigger or smaller the image becomes.


Identifying a Dilation About the Origin

A dilation keeps the same shape and angle sizes, but it changes the size of the figure. When the dilation is from the origin, all points move closer to or farther from (0, 0). Here’s what you’ll notice:

  • A scale factor greater than 1 means the image is larger than the original.

  • A scale factor between 0 and 1 means the image is smaller than the original.

Enlargement Dilation

The figure is enlarged where both the x- and y-coordinates of each vertice are multipled by the scale factor.

Reduction Dilation

The figure is reduction where both the x- and y-coordinates of each vertice are multipled by the scale factor.

Example:

ABC was dilated by a factor of 2.

Example:

ABC was dilated by a factor of \(\frac{1}{2}\).



Identifying a Dilation About the Origin

To recognize a dilation:

  • Compare each point’s distance from the origin before and after the transformation.

  • If the image is along the same line from the origin but a different distance, it’s likely a dilation.



Example 1

Triangle ABC and its transformed image A'B'C' are shown.

What transformation occurred?

A) Translation 3 units right
B) Rotation 90° about the origin
C) Dilation about the origin with scale factor 3
D) Reflection over the x-axis

Explanation: Each point is twice as far from the origin as it was before. That means the figure has been dilated from the origin with a scale factor of 3.

We can look at dividing x' by x and y' by y for each coordinate to identify the scale factor.

\(A(-1,1)\) and \(A'(-3,3)\). If we look at \(\frac{x'}{x}\) we get \(\frac{-3}{-1}=3\) for x and \(\frac{3}{1}=3\) for y.

This tells us the scale factor is 3.

Answer: Option C



Example 2

Quadrilateral ABCD and its transformed image A'B'C'D' are shown.

What transformation occurred?

A) Dilation about the origin with scale factor \(\frac{2}{3}\)
B) Translation 3 units left
C) Rotation 180° about the origin
D) Reflection over the y-axis

Explanation: Each point in the image is two-thirds the distance from the origin compared to the original. That means the figure has been reduced using a scale factor of \(\frac{2}{3}\).

We can look at dividing x' by x and y' by y for each coordinate to identify the scale factor.

\(B(6,6)\) and \(B'(4,4)\). If we look at \(\frac{x'}{x}\) we get \(\frac{4}{6}=\frac{2}{3}\) for x and \(\frac{4}{6}=\frac{2}{3}\) for y.

This tells us the scale factor is 3.

Answer: Option A



Drawing a Dilation From the Origin

To draw a dilation from the origin:

  1. Identify the scale factor (how much bigger or smaller the figure should be).

  2. Start with one point.

    • Count how far it is from the origin (both x and y).

    • Multiply both the x- and y-values by the scale factor.

    • Plot the new point.

  3. Repeat for every vertex of the figure.

  4. Use the polygon tool to connect the new points in the same order.

Helpful Tip: A dilation keeps the same shape and orientation, just at a different size and position from the origin.



Example 3

Triangle ABC, shown, is dilated by a scale factor of 1.5.

Draw the dilated image A'B'C'.

Solution:

  • Multiply the x- and y-coordinates of each vertex by 1.5 to get the coordinates of the dilated figure.
    • \(A(-4,0)\rightarrow A'(1.5\cdot -4,1.5\cdot 0)=(-6,0)\)
    • \(B(3,4)\rightarrow B'(1.5\cdot 3, 1.5\cdot 4)=(4.5,6)\)
    • \(C(2,-3)\rightarrow C'(1.5\cdot 2, 1.5\cdot -3)=(3,-4.5)\)
  • Plot each coordinate of the dilated figure.
  • Use the polygon tool to draw the new image.
  • Note that the shape and orientation of the triangle did not change, only the size of it. Also, the corresponding sides are parallel.



Example 4

Quadrilateral ABCD, shown, is dilated by a scale factor of \(\frac{2}{5}\).

Draw the dilated image A'B'C'D'.

Solution:

  • Multiply the x- and y-coordinates of each vertex by \(\frac{2}{5}\) to get the coordinates of the dilated figure.
    • \(A(-5,0)\rightarrow A'(\frac{2}{5}(-5), \frac{2}{5}(0))=(-2,0)\)
    • \(B(10,10)\rightarrow B'(\frac{2}{5}(10), \frac{2}{5}(10))=(4,4)\)
    • \(C(15,5)\rightarrow C'(\frac{2}{5}(15), \frac{2}{5}(5))=(6,2)\)
    • \(D(0,-5)\rightarrow D'(\frac{2}{5}(0), \frac{2}{5}(-5))=(0,-2)\)
  • Plot each coordinate of the dilated figure.
  • Use the polygon tool to draw the new image.
  • Note that the shape and orientation of the quadrilateral did not change, only the size of it. Also, the corresponding sides are parallel.



Conclusion

Today, you learned how to identify and draw dilations about the origin. You practiced recognizing when a figure grows or shrinks while keeping its shape, and you described how the coordinates change based on how far they move from the origin. A dilation with a scale factor greater than 1 makes the shape larger, and a scale factor between 0 and 1 makes it smaller. By observing patterns and multiplying distances from the origin, you can describe and create dilated figures without using any algebraic rules—just by thinking and looking carefully at the coordinate plane!
