📚 VOCABULARY

Center of Rotation: The fixed point around which a figure turns during a rotation.

Coordinate Plane: A two-dimensional surface formed by the intersection of a horizontal number line (x-axis) and a vertical number line (y-axis).

Dilation: A transformation that changes the size of a figure but not its shape, either enlarging or reducing it based on a scale factor.

Image: The resulting figure after a transformation is applied to the original figure.

Origin: The point where the x-axis and y-axis intersect on the coordinate plane, located at (0,0).

Preimage: The original figure before any transformation has been applied.

Reflection: A transformation that "flips" a figure over a line, creating a mirror image.

Rigid Transformation: A transformation that changes a figure’s position but not its size or shape (translations, rotations, and reflections are rigid transformations).

Rotation: A transformation that turns a figure around a fixed point, called the center of rotation.

Scale Factor: A number that describes how much a figure is enlarged or reduced during a dilation.

Translation: A transformation that slides a figure from one location to another without turning or flipping it.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

In today’s lesson, we will explore how to identify and draw rotations on the coordinate plane. You will learn how to recognize when a figure has been rotated 90, 180, or 270 degrees either clockwise or counterclockwise around the origin (0, 0). We won’t be using algebraic rules or formulas—instead, we’ll focus on noticing how the coordinates of the figure change and describing what happens to each point. You’ll get to practice identifying the type of rotation and try drawing rotated shapes yourself using clear, geometric thinking.


Identifying Rotations: 90°, 180°, and 270° Around the Origin

When a figure is rotated about the origin, it spins around the center point (0, 0), but its size and shape do not change.

Rotated \(90^\circ\) Counterclockwise about the origin

(Same as \(270^\circ\) clockwise)

The figure turns one-quarter turn to the left.

  • The x- and y-values switch places, but the new x-value becomes negative.

Rotated \(180^\circ\) Counterclockwise about the origin

(Same as \(180^\circ\) clockwise)

The figure turns halfway around the origin.

  • Both the x- and y-values change to their opposites.

Rotated \(270^\circ\) Counterclockwise about the origin

(Same as \(90^\circ\) clockwise)

The figure turns one-quarter turn to the right.

  • Each point switches its x- and y-values, but the new y-value becomes negative.

Example 1

Triangle ABC is transformed to create triangle A'B'C' as shown.

What transformation occurred?

A) 270° clockwise rotation about the origin
B) Reflection over the y-axis
C) Translation 3 units left and 1 unit up
D) 180° rotation about the origin

Solution:

  • If we observe what happened to each coordinate and the triangle over all, we notice that it appears to have rotated a quarter of a turn to the left.
  • This is a rotation of a 90 degree counterclockwise which is the same as 270 degrees clockwise.

Answer: Option A



Example 2

Rectangle ABCG is transformed to create rectangle A'B'C'G' as shown.

What transformation occurred?

A) 90° Clockwise Rotation
B) 180° Rotation about the origin
C) Reflection over the x-axis
D) Translation 6 units up

Solution:

  • If we observe what happened to each coordinate and the rectangle over all, we notice that it appears to have rotated halfway around the origin.
  • This is a rotation of a 180 degrees counterclockwise or clockwise. Direction does not matter with 180 degree rotations.

Answer: Option B



Drawing Rotations on the Coordinate Plane

To draw a rotation about the origin, you do not need to memorize any algebraic formulas. Instead, think about how the shape would turn on a grid if it were connected to the origin like a spinning pinwheel. Each point turns the same amount around the center point (0, 0).

Here are some tips:

  • For 90° clockwise, points move one quarter turn to the right.

    • Switch the x- and y-coordinates.

    • Then, change the new y-coordinate to its opposite (make it negative if it was positive, or positive if it was negative).

  • For 180°, imagine turning your paper halfway around.

    • Change both the x- and y-coordinates to their opposites.

    • Positive numbers become negative, and negative numbers become positive.

  • For 270° clockwise (or 90° counterclockwise), turn one quarter to the left.

    • Switch the x- and y-coordinates.

    • Then, change the new x-coordinate to its opposite.



Example 3

Quadrilateral ABCG on the graph is rotated 270 degrees counterclockwise.

Draw the rotated image A'B'C'G'.

Solution:

  • For 270° counterclockwise, points move one quarter turn to the right.

    • Switch the x- and y-coordinates.

    • Then, change the new y-coordinate to its opposite (make it negative if it was positive, or positive if it was negative).

  • It will be helpful to apply the transformation to each point and find what each coordinate should be.
    • \(A(4,7)\) becomes \(A'(7,-4)\)
    • \(B(1,9)\) becomes \(B'(9,-1)\)
    • \(C(-2,5)\) becomes \(C'(5,2)\)
    • \(G(1,3)\) becomes \(G'(3,-1)\)
  • Then plot each point for A'B'C'G'.
  • Then connect the points to form the quadrilateral.

 

Example 4

Triangle ABC on the graph is rotated 90 degrees counterclockwise.

Draw the rotated image A'B'C'.

Solution:

  • For 90° counterclockwise, points move one quarter turn to the left.

    • Switch the x- and y-coordinates.

    • Then, change the new x-coordinate to its opposite.

  • It will be helpful to apply the transformation to each point and find what each coordinate should be.
    • \(A(4,1)\) becomes \(A'(-1,4)\)
    • \(B(-1,5)\) becomes \(B'(-5,-1)\)
    • \(C(-4,1)\) becomes \(C'(-1,-4)\)
  • Then plot each point for A'B'C'G'.
  • Then connect the points to form the quadrilateral.



Conclusion

In this lesson, you learned how to identify and draw rotations of 90°, 180°, or 270° about the origin without using any formulas or rules. You focused on observing how the coordinates of a shape change as it spins around the center point of the coordinate plane. Whether identifying a rotation or drawing one yourself, always keep the origin as your anchor and notice how each point moves. With practice, you’ll be able to recognize and apply these rotations with confidence and clarity.
