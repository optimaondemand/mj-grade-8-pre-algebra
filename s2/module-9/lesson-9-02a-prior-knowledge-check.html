What is Experimental Probability?

Experimental probability is determined by conducting an experiment and recording the outcomes. It is calculated by dividing the number of times an event occurs by the total number of trials in the experiment. Unlike theoretical probability, which is based on possible outcomes, experimental probability is based on actual outcomes.

Theoretical vs. Experimental Probability

  • Theoretical Probability is what we expect to happen, based on all possible outcomes.
  • Experimental Probability is what actually happens when we perform the experiment.

Theoretical probability gives us an expectation, while experimental probability provides us with real-world results. Over many trials, experimental probabilities tend to get closer to theoretical probabilities, illustrating the Law of Large Numbers.

Connecting Theoretical and Experimental Probability

Theoretical probability helps us predict the outcomes before we conduct an experiment. When we perform an experiment multiple times, we can compare the experimental probability to the theoretical probability. To get experimental probabilities closer to theoretical probabilities, we can increase the number of trials in our experiments.

Conclusion

Experimental probability provides insight into how probability plays out in real-world situations, while theoretical probability offers a baseline expectation. By understanding both, we can better analyze and predict outcomes in various scenarios. Remember, the more trials you perform, the closer your experimental probability will likely get to the theoretical probability. This relationship between theory and experiment is a foundational concept in the study of probability, bridging the gap between mathematics and the real world.
