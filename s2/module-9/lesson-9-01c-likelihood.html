📚 VOCABULARY

Certain: When we say an event is certain, it means it's 100% going to happen. There's no doubt about it. For example, if you drop a ball, it's certain it will fall to the ground because of gravity.

Event: An event is any result that happens. In math, it's something we're interested in when we're doing an experiment or looking at probabilities. For example, in flipping a coin, having it land on heads is an event.

Experiment: An experiment is a test or trial you do to see what happens. It's like a science experiment but for math. You do something (like rolling a dice) and watch to see what the outcome is (which number faces up).

Impossible: If something is impossible, it means there's a 0% chance of it happening. It just can't happen. For example, rolling a regular dice and getting a 7 is impossible because the dice only have numbers 1 through 6.

Likelihood: Likelihood is just another word for how probable something is, or how likely it is to happen. If something has a high likelihood, it means it's very probable it will happen.

Likely: If we say an event is likely, it means there's a good chance it will happen. It's more than 50% likely. For example, if you have a bag full of red and only a few blue marbles, it's likely you'll pick a red marble.

Outcome: An outcome is the result of an experiment. Whatever happens as a result of the experiment is called the outcome. For example, if you roll a dice and it lands on 4, the outcome is 4.

Sample Space: The sample space is all the possible outcomes you can get from an experiment. If you're rolling a dice, the sample space is 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6 because those are all the results you could possibly get.

Theoretical Probability: This is the probability you get by thinking through an experiment logically, without actually doing the experiment. It's what should happen in theory. For example, the theoretical probability of flipping a coin and getting heads is 1/2, because there are two sides to the coin, and one of them is heads.

Unlikely: If something is unlikely, it means it probably won't happen. It's less than 50% likely. For example, if you have a bag with mostly red marbles and only a few blue ones, it's unlikely you'll pick a blue marble.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

In this lesson, we will explore the concept of likelihood and how it is used in probability to describe how probable it is that an event will happen. Likelihood is essentially the chance of an event occurring, and it can be measured on a scale from "impossible" to "certain." This scale helps us quantify and communicate our understanding of risk and chance in everyday situations and mathematical problems.


Let's begin by looking at what likelihood is and the likelihood scale. Then watch a short video on the likelihood of events.

The Likelihood Scale:

  • Impossible (0% chance): The event cannot happen.
  • Unlikely (<50% chance): The event is not expected to happen most of the time.
  • As Likely as Unlikely (50% chance): The event has an equal chance of happening or not happening.
  • Likely (>50% chance but <100%): The event is expected to happen most of the time.
  • Certain (100% chance): The event will definitely happen.

Example 3

Suppose you are conducting an experiment where you are going to roll an 8-sided die and then flip a coin.

What is the likelihood of rolling an even number and then landing on heads?

Solution:

  • Let's begin by looking at the theoretical probability of the event.
  • Since the event of rolling an even number and then landing on heads has a 50% chance of happening, then the event is as likely as unlikely to happen.
  • This is As Likely as Unlikely because there's an equal chance of rolling an even or odd number and getting heads or tails.


Example 4

Suppose you are drawing a marble from a bag of marbles that contains only blue marbles.

What is the likelihood of drawing a blue marble?

Solution:

  • Since the bag only contains blue marbles and we want to draw a marble, there is a 100% chance of drawing a blue marble.
  • This is Certain because no matter what, you will draw a blue marble.


Conclusion

Today, we've learned about the concept of likelihood and how it's represented on a scale from impossible to certain. By applying this scale to various examples, we can better understand and communicate the probability of different events occurring. Understanding the likelihood of events helps us make informed decisions and predictions in both everyday life and mathematical problems. Remember, the likelihood scale is a powerful tool for interpreting the chances of various outcomes.
