📖 PRIOR KNOWLEDGE

In a previous grade, you learned about determining the likelihood of a simple experiment and what likelihood means. As we get ready to explore likelihood of repeated experiments and understanding theoretical probability of repeated experiments, it is important to review what likelihood is and what it means for simple experiments.

Probability Scale

The probability of an event tells us how likely it is to happen and is usually expressed as a percentage (%). Here’s what each term means:

  • Impossible (0%): The event cannot happen.
  • Unlikely (<50%): The event is not expected to happen most of the time.
  • As Likely as Unlikely (50%): The event has an equal chance of happening or not happening.
  • Likely (>50%): The event is expected to happen most of the time.
  • Certain (100%): The event will definitely happen.

(myrec)


Probability Notation

Probability of an event occurring is expressed in the following notation P(event). For example, the probability of rolling a 5 on a standard six-sided dice is written as P(rolling a 5).

A standard 6-sided dice has 6 faces each numbered 1-6. So there is one 5 out of 6 numbers on the dice. This would be a low percentage of chance to roll a 5.


Now, let's review some examples.

Example 1

Imagine a spinner divided into equal parts, each marked with a different outcome. The chance of the spinner landing on any given section is determined by the size of the section relative to the whole spinner.

Suppose you have a spinner divided into 5 equal sections: red, blue, green, purple, and pink.

What is the likelihood of spinning and landing on the color purple?

  • P(spinning purple) - Since there is only 1 purple section out of 5, this is less than half.
  • P(spinning purple) < 50% - Unlikely 
    • This event is considered unlikely on the probability scale.
    • This does not mean it won't happen, it just has a low probability since there are 5 equal sections and purple only represents one section.

What is the likelihood of landing on red, blue, or green?

  • P(spinning red, blue, or green) - these 3 colors represent more than half the colors and sections on the spinner.
  • P(spinning red, blue, or green) > 50% - Likely 
    • Landing on one of those colors is likely on the probability scale since it is more than 50%.

What is the likelihood of spinning and landing on the color yellow?

  • P(spinning yellow) - There are no yellow sections on the spinner
  • P(spinning yellow) = 0% - Impossible 
    • Since yellow does not exist on this spinner, it is impossible to spin and land on that color.

What is the likelihood of spinning any color on the spinner?

  • P(blue, green, purple, pink, or red) - represents all the colors on the spinner
  • P(blue, green, purple, pink, or red) = 100% - Certain
    • Since this represents the probability of landing on any of the sections, we are certain to land on a color.


Example 2

Imagine you have a bag of marbles. You have 12 red marbles, 6 blue marbles, 5 yellow marbles, and 2 green marbles. A total of 22 marbles.

What is the likelihood of drawing out a green marble?

  • P(green marble) - There are only 2 marbles out of 22 marbles. This is less than half the marbles.
  • P(green)< 50% - Unlikely

What is the likelihood of drawing out a red marble?

  • P(red marble) - There are 12 red marbles out of the 22 total. This is more than half the marbles.
  • P(red)>50% - Likely

What is the likelihood of drawing out a blue or yellow marble?

  • P(blue or yellow marble) - There are 11 marbles that are blue or yellow of the 22 total. This is exactly half the marbles.
  • P(blue or yellow)=50% - as likely as unlikely


Conclusion

Understanding how to calculate and compare probabilities helps you make informed predictions about outcomes. Remember, the key terms (impossible, unlikely, as likely as unlikely, likely, and certain) help you quickly assess how probable an event is. Keep practicing with different spinners or scenarios to become more comfortable with these concepts!
