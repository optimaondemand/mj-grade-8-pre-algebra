📚 VOCABULARY

Event: An event is a specific result or outcome that we're interested in when we perform an experiment in probability. For example, if you flip a coin, getting a "heads" is an event.

Experimental Probability: This is the probability calculated from actually doing an experiment and observing what happens. It's found by dividing the number of times the event happens by the total number of trials (tries or attempts). For instance, if you flip a coin 100 times and get heads 55 times, the experimental probability of getting heads is 55/100 or 55%.

Frequency: Frequency is how often something happens. In probability, it refers to the number of times an event occurs within a certain number of trials or experiments.

Law of Large Numbers: This law states that as you perform an experiment more and more times, the experimental probability (what you observe happening) gets closer to the theoretical probability (what should happen according to math).

Outcome: An outcome is the result of a single trial of an experiment. If you roll a die, the outcome is the number that comes up.

Prediction: A prediction is a guess about what might happen in the future, based on data, patterns, or probabilities. For example, if a coin has come up heads 9 times in a row, you might predict that tails is "due" (though probability-wise, each flip is still independent!).

Probability: Probability measures how likely it is for an event to occur, usually expressed as a fraction, percentage, or decimal. It ranges from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain).

Repeated Experiment: This is when you perform the same experiment multiple times to observe different outcomes and better understand the probability of various events.

Sample Size: In probability and statistics, the sample size is the number of observations or trials in an experiment. A larger sample size can give more reliable results.

Sample Space: The sample space is the set of all possible outcomes of an experiment. For a 6-sided die, the sample space is {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}.

Simple Experiment: A simple experiment is one in which there's a single action performed, and it has a definite number of possible outcomes. Flipping a coin is a simple experiment because the outcomes are just heads or tails.

Theoretical Probability: This is the probability of an event calculated based on the possible outcomes, without actually performing the experiment. It's what mathematically "should" happen. For instance, the theoretical probability of rolling a 3 on a 6-sided die is 1/6, because there is one 3 and six possible outcomes.

In today's lesson, where we'll explore the world of probability and learn how to make predictions based on theoretical probabilities in simple experiments. A prediction is our best guess about what will happen in the future, based on the information we have now. In probability, this information comes from understanding the likelihood, or theoretical probability, of specific events.


What is Theoretical Probability?

Theoretical probability is the chance of an event happening when all outcomes are equally likely. It’s calculated by dividing the number of favorable outcomes by the total number of possible outcomes in a simple experiment.


How to Use Theoretical Probability for Making Predictions

Once we know the theoretical probability of an event, we can use it to make predictions about how often that event will occur over a series of trials. If an experiment is repeated many times, we expect the actual outcomes to start matching our theoretical probabilities.
