📚 VOCABULARY

Certain: When we say an event is certain, it means it's 100% going to happen. There's no doubt about it. For example, if you drop a ball, it's certain it will fall to the ground because of gravity.

Event: An event is any result that happens. In math, it's something we're interested in when we're doing an experiment or looking at probabilities. For example, in flipping a coin, having it land on heads is an event.

Experiment: An experiment is a test or trial you do to see what happens. It's like a science experiment but for math. You do something (like rolling a dice) and watch to see what the outcome is (which number faces up).

Impossible: If something is impossible, it means there's a 0% chance of it happening. It just can't happen. For example, rolling a regular dice and getting a 7 is impossible because the dice only have numbers 1 through 6.

Likelihood: Likelihood is just another word for how probable something is, or how likely it is to happen. If something has a high likelihood, it means it's very probable it will happen.

Likely: If we say an event is likely, it means there's a good chance it will happen. It's more than 50% likely. For example, if you have a bag full of red and only a few blue marbles, it's likely you'll pick a red marble.

Outcome: An outcome is the result of an experiment. Whatever happens as a result of the experiment is called the outcome. For example, if you roll a dice and it lands on 4, the outcome is 4.

Sample Space: The sample space is all the possible outcomes you can get from an experiment. If you're rolling a dice, the sample space is 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6 because those are all the results you could possibly get.

Theoretical Probability: This is the probability you get by thinking through an experiment logically, without actually doing the experiment. It's what should happen in theory. For example, the theoretical probability of flipping a coin and getting heads is 1/2, because there are two sides to the coin, and one of them is heads.

Unlikely: If something is unlikely, it means it probably won't happen. It's less than 50% likely. For example, if you have a bag with mostly red marbles and only a few blue ones, it's unlikely you'll pick a blue marble.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

In today's lesson, we will explore the concept of sample spaces in the context of probability. Understanding how to find the sample space—the set of all possible outcomes—of experiments is crucial whether you're dealing with repeated events or experiments involving two different events. Whether you're rolling dice, flipping coins, or drawing marbles from a bag, identifying the sample space is your first step in analyzing any probability experiment. Let's dive into some examples to clarify these ideas.


A sample space is a term in probability that refers to the set of all possible outcomes of an experiment. Think of it as the big picture of every result that could possibly happen when you conduct an experiment, no matter how simple or complex it is.

 

Why is Sample Space Important?

Understanding the sample space is crucial because it lays the groundwork for calculating probabilities. Before we can determine how likely an event is, we need to know all the outcomes that could occur. This comprehensive overview ensures that our probability calculations are accurate and that we haven't overlooked any possibilities.


Example 1

Imagine you have a bag containing 4 blue marbles, 3 red marbles, and 2 green marbles. You decide to draw a marble, observe its color, and then return it to the bag before drawing again.

What is the sample space of the experiment?

Solution:

  • Since the marble is replaced each time, the sample space for drawing once includes all colors available: blue (B), red (R), and green (G). For two draws, we combine these possibilities: BB, BR, BG, RB, RR, RG, GB, GR, and GG. Each pair represents a possible outcome from two draws, showing the importance of considering every combination.
  • We write the sample space as the set of all possible outcomes.


Example 2

You're rolling a standard 6-sided die two consecutive times. What is the sample space of this repeated experiment?

Solution:

  • Here, the sample space includes all the possible outcomes for each roll, combined in pairs for the second roll. This results in 36 unique combinations (1,1), (1,2), …, (6,6), illustrating the exponential growth of possibilities with each repeated action.
  • We can write it as a set of all possible outcomes.


Example 3

You draw a marble from a bag with 4 pink, 2 blue, and 1 yellow marble, then spin a spinner divided into four sections labeled A, B, C, and D.

What is the sample space for this experiment with 2 events?

Solution:

  • This example shows how sample spaces expand when combining different types of experiments. The sample space includes all possible outcomes of the two actions, drawing a colored marble, then spinning the spinner, demonstrating how to account for each event's outcomes. Since we have colors and letters in this experiment, it might be easier to use the color's name to help keep track of our sample space.
  • The sample space will include the outcomes of drawing a marble, and then spinning the spinner; like pink,A, pink,B, pink,C, pink,D, blue,A, blue, B, etc... Let's write the set of all possible outcomes.


Conclusion

Today, we've unraveled the concept of sample spaces, the foundation upon which probability theory is built. Understanding and identifying the sample space of any experiment is crucial because it allows us to see every possible outcome. This comprehensive perspective is essential for accurately calculating probabilities and making informed predictions about various events. By mastering sample spaces, you equip yourself with a fundamental tool in probability, opening doors to deeper analysis and understanding of the world's random phenomena.
