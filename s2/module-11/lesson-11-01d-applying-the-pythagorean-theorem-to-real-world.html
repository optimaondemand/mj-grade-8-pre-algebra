📚 VOCABULARY

Converse of the Pythagorean Theorem: The Converse of the Pythagorean Theorem states that if the square of the longest side of a triangle (often called the hypotenuse) equals the sum of the squares of the other two sides, then the triangle is a right triangle. In other words, this rule helps us determine if a triangle with certain side lengths can be a right triangle.

Coordinate: A coordinate is a number used to identify the location of a point on a graph or grid. It is part of a set of numbers used in a coordinate system, like the x-coordinate and y-coordinate on a coordinate plane, which show positions along the horizontal and vertical axes, respectively.

Coordinate Plane: The coordinate plane is a two-dimensional surface on which each point is determined by a pair of numbers, called coordinates. These coordinates correspond to distances along two perpendicular lines or axes: the horizontal axis (x-axis) and the vertical axis (y-axis).

Distance: Distance is a measure of how far apart two points or objects are from each other. In mathematics, it's often calculated as the straight line between two points in a space, like on a coordinate plane.

Hypotenuse: The hypotenuse is the longest side of a right triangle, lying opposite the right angle. It is the side used in the Pythagorean Theorem to find the relationship between the lengths of the sides of a right triangle.

Leg: In the context of right triangles, a leg refers to one of the two sides that form the right angle. There are always two legs in a right triangle, and they meet to form the right angle.

Pythagorean Theorem: The Pythagorean Theorem is a fundamental principle in geometry which states that in a right triangle, the square of the length of the hypotenuse (the side opposite the right angle) is equal to the sum of the squares of the lengths of the other two sides (the legs). This theorem is essential for calculating distances and understanding right triangles.

Right Triangle: A right triangle is a type of triangle that has one angle measuring exactly 90 degrees, known as the right angle. The sides forming this right angle are called the legs, and the side opposite the right angle is the hypotenuse.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

In today’s lesson, we will explore how to apply the Distance Formula to real-world situations. You already know how to find the distance between two points on a coordinate plane using the Pythagorean Theorem. Now, you will use that knowledge to solve problems that you might encounter outside of the classroom. Whether you are mapping a path between two locations or designing something in real life, understanding how to find distances on a coordinate plane is a useful and important skill.


Using the Pythagorean Theorem as the Distance Formula

The Pythagorean Theorem states that for any right triangle, the relationship between the lengths of the sides is:

\(a^2+b^2=c^2\)

where \(a\) and \(b\) are the lengths of the legs and \(c\) is the length of the hypotenuse.

On a coordinate plane:

  • The horizontal distance is the absolute difference between the x-coordinates: \(|x_2-x_1|\)

  • The vertical distance is the absolute difference between the y-coordinates: \(|y_2-y_1|\)

  • These two distances form the legs of a right triangle, and the distance between the two points is the hypotenuse.



Example 1

You are helping to design a new park. Two important points in the park are the location of a playground and a water fountain.

On a grid representing the park, the playground is at \((2,3)\) and the water fountain is at \((10,8)\).

Find the distance between the playground and the water fountain. Round to the nearest hundredth if necessary.

Solution:

  • You can choose to graph the two points on a coordinate plane and use the method shown above, or you can work through it algebraically.
  • We begin by finding the horizontal distance between the two points.
    • We can do this by counting on the grid or using the formula \(|x_2-x_1|\).
    • Name \(x_1=2\) and \(x_2=10\)
    • \(|10-2|\implies |8|\implies 8\)
    • We find the horizontal distance to be 8 units.
  • Next, we find the vertical distance between the two points.
    • Name \(y_1=3\) and \(y_2=8\)
    • \(|8-3|\implies |5|\implies 5\)
    • We find the vertical distance to be 5 units.
  • Now, we use these two distances as the values of the legs of the right triangle formed on the coordinate plane to find the diagonal distance between the two points.
  • \(8^2+5^2 \implies 64+25 \implies 89\)
  • \(c^2=89 \implies c=\sqrt{89}\implies c\approx 9.43398...\)

The approximate distance between the playground and the water fountain is 9.43 units.


Example 2

You are planning a drone flight across a field.

The starting path is at \((-4,1)\) and the ending point is at \((3,-2)\).

Find the distance the drone will fly. Round to the nearest hundredth if necessary.

Solution:

  • You can choose to graph the two points on a coordinate plane and use the method shown above, or you can work through it algebraically.
  • We begin by finding the horizontal distance between the two points.
    • We can do this by counting on the grid or using the formula \(|x_2-x_1|\).
    • Name \(x_1=-4\) and \(x_2=3\)
    • \(|3-(-4)|\implies |3+4|\implies 7\)
    • We find the horizontal distance to be 7 units.
  • Next, we find the vertical distance between the two points.
    • Name \(y_1=1\) and \(y_2=-2\)
    • \(|-2-1|\implies |-3|\implies 3\)
    • We find the vertical distance to be 3 units.
  • Now, we use these two distances as the values of the legs of the right triangle formed on the coordinate plane to find the diagonal distance between the two points.
  • \(7^2+3^2 \implies 49+9 \implies 58\)
  • \(c^2=58 \implies c=\sqrt{58}\implies c\approx 7.615773...\)

The approximate distance the drone will fly is 7.62 units.



Example 3

A kite is flying 18.5 meters in the air, and the string tied to the kite extends 30 meters from Sofia’s hand to the kite.

How far horizontally (ground distance) is Sofia standing from the point directly under the kite?

Solution:

  • We can begin by drawing an image to represent the situation so we can help identify what we have and what we are looking to solve for.
  • From the drawing, we notice that we are looking for a leg of the right triangle. We are given the hypotenuse and another leg.
  • So, we fill in what we know into the Pythagorean theorem and solve for what we do not know.
  • \(18.5^2+b^2=30^2 \implies b^2=30^2-18.5^2 \implies b=\sqrt{30^2-18.5^2} \implies b\approx 35.25m\)

The distance from Sofia's hand to the point directly underneath the kit is approximately 35.25 meters.

 



Conclusion

Today, you learned how to use the Distance Formula to solve real-world problems. By thinking of locations as points on a grid, you can find the distance between them using the same methods we practiced in earlier lessons. Whether designing a park or planning a drone flight, applying mathematical concepts like the Distance Formula helps you solve real-life challenges efficiently and accurately. As you continue practicing, you’ll see just how useful math can be in everyday life!
